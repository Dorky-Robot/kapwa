Collectives & kita

kita is what a collective knows. kapwa is what collectives owe each other. They are the same idea at two scales — an append-only log of attributed acts, the present derived by replay — and they are complements that must not overlap. That division is what lets a whole practice or brokerage act as one participant.

kapwa knows nothing about kita. Everything on this page is kita, and the applications built on it, using kapwa through two generic slots any protocol may use: a reference and a typed payload (riders). The treaty below is a promise about scope, not a dependency in either direction.

The treaty

One question sorts any feature: is it about what we know, or about what we owe?

kita — what a collective knowskapwa — what collectives owe each other
livesinside one collectivebetween collectives, and between the agents and people in them
alwayscontent with identity; statements between things; questions answered with citationscommitments, handoffs, questions for a human, pointers
nevercrosses a boundary · replicates to another collective · routes anything · knows another kita existsstores content · answers "what do we know" · holds a record or PHI · grows a graph or a query language
orderone writer's order, one logmany logs, merged by (at, writer, seq)
trusteveryone inside is trusted; authentication is the application'snobody outside is trusted; every line is signed

The seam is exactly three things

1 · Participants

A kapwa identity names a participant in some collective's kita. The collective is itself a participant — in its own kita and in its neighbours'.

2 · References — bare, or with an excerpt

["ref","kita:monica:d310","about"] points home (to kapwa it is just a URI it doesn't understand). When the receiver needs to see, the sender's application may attach the ring as a kita act; kapwa carries it unread (below).

3 · Envelope words

Who, when, what produced it: kita's who · at · source, kapwa's by · at · source. Same meanings, so a bridge is a rename, not a translation.

Anything that wants a fourth seam is a sign one protocol is growing into the other.

kita2 in eight lines

  • Two things, five operations, one door. Nodes and statements; make · unmake · change · say · unsay; land(act) is the only way in.
  • An act lands whole or is refused whole. Refusals are structural, never about meaning.
  • The log is the only storage; the graph is the log replayed.
  • Every act carries an envelope: who (a node id), at, source (the tool that built it).
  • An act's id is a hash of its contents — delivered twice, applied once.
  • No domain nouns, anywhere. A practice, a household, a firm and a brokerage are one product wearing different words.
  • No UI; authentication is the application's, permanently.
  • One kita per collective. Store::share lets several programs on one machine share it; nothing crosses machines yet.

The detail that matters here

everyday-vet-admin's README: "A kita born empty gets its practice participant at first start (from EV_PRACTICE_NAME)."

The collective is already a participant in its own kita. "Everyday Vet" is a node, exactly like Sara is a node. Things can be said about it, and acts can be attributed to it. kita never distinguished a person from a group — a participant is just "a named dot that things get said about."

That is the hook. kapwa doesn't need a concept of "organization." It needs to let a participant be a collective.

A collective is three things

"As one": the writer is the collective, by is the member

kapwa already separates whose log it landed in from who acted. Today writer is a machine. Let it be a collective, and the outside world sees one voice while the inside keeps attribution:

What Dorky Robot sees

everyday-vet said  4a1c8
  "SMS consent text needs the clinic's legal name"
  → to dorkyrobot

dorkyrobot took it            # (claude)
dorkyrobot done: "updated; see PR 212"
everyday-vet 👍               # (sara)

You address the practice, not Sara. Whoever is on duty there picks it up — a person or the practice's agent. The member is shown small, or not at all.

The event underneath

{"writer":"everyday-vet","seq":88,"at":"…",
 "by":"sara",
 "kind":"note","id":"4a1c8",
 "note":"SMS consent text needs the clinic's legal name",
 "tags":[["to","dorkyrobot"],
         ["ref","kita:ev:d2877"]]}

Nothing new in the protocol: writer names the collective, by the member, and two tags carry the addressee and a pointer home.

Inside a collective with several machines (yours), each machine keeps its own log — that's what makes offline writing safe — and the writer name carries both: dorkyrobot/mini, dorkyrobot/mac2024. Nodes of one collective trust each other fully; across collectives, scoped. Anything that renders a board folds dorkyrobot/* into one voice.

The bridge does two things

The seams are what the two protocols share. The bridge is the small piece of each application that uses them. It lives in the application, because authentication and "what may leave" are the application's.

Lands what crossed

kapwa events that involve this collective land as ordinary acts: source:"kapwa", who = the remote collective's participant, a dot with the text, about the things it points at. Then "what did we promise Dorky Robot?" is an ask, and chat can cite it.

Idempotent both ways: acts are content-addressed, events are (writer, seq). The bridge can crash and re-run.

Speaks out

A convention, not a mechanism: an act that says say(d, "ask", other_collective) is something the application may publish as a kapwa say --to, with a reference back to d and, when the receiver needs to see, the ring around it as a parcel.

Carrying context: kapwa carries kita's grammar, not kita's meaning

kita's whole grammar is one shape: say(from, word, to), with an open vocabulary — "any word you use is valid the moment you use it." kapwa already has a slot with that shape: a tag. And kita already has a self-contained, plain-JSON unit for "some things and how they connect": an act. So:

1 · A reference may carry a word relations

A tag that points at something can say how it relates. Subject is always "this item"; the word is free; the object is an item, a participant, or a dot in somebody's kita.

["e", "a81b…", "depends-on"]     # item → item
["e", "77c0…", "duplicates"]
["p", "5d30…", "decided-by"]     # item → participant
["ref", "kita:monica:d310", "about"] # item → a dot at home

This is nostr's existing "marker" position on a tag, used with kita's rule for words. No list of allowed words in the protocol — Berners-Lee's "two people never chose the same keywords." The fold understands two or three (depends-on is what makes ready mean something) and keeps the rest.

2 · A payload may be a kita act a parcel of context

When pointers aren't enough — the receiver can't see inside your kita — send the neighbourhood itself: the ring around the subject, written as an ordinary act of make and say. kernel.md: an act is "plain data — the same shape in memory, on the wire, and as one line in the log."

kapwa doesn't read it. "Unknown kinds and fields are kept, and ignored" was already the rule; a parcel is just content kapwa has no opinion about.

Every event carries a sentence a person or an agent can read without any rider installed. The typed payload rides beside it as a string kapwa stores and never opens.

What each side does with it

The sender's application

Picks the subject, takes its ring to the depth that matters, leaves out what shouldn't travel, and compiles it to an act. That choice is authorship: the parcel is something the collective deliberately said outward, signed, and accountable for.

kapwa

Signs, stores, replicates, shows "has context (5 things)". It never indexes, queries, folds or validates a parcel. Delete kita from the world and kapwa behaves identically.

The receiver

An agent reads it as-is: five makes and five says is about as legible as context gets for a model. An application may land it in its own kita — source:"kapwa", who = the sender's participant — so it's connected to everything else it knows about Monica's brokerage. Or it may not. kapwa doesn't care.

The honest tension: a parcel is a copy

kita exists because copies drift; Nelson's line is its creed. A parcel is a copy that crosses a boundary. Three things keep it honest:

rulewhy
Ids keep their home. monica:d310, never a fresh local idLanded twice, it's the same node (acts are idempotent). And everyone can tell it is a view of a foreign dot, not a native one.
Corrections can follow. A later parcel with change monica:d310 … updates every place it landed"Correct it in one place" extends across the boundary for as long as the sender keeps saying so. Whether the receiver keeps listening is its application's choice, not a protocol feature.
Parcels are additive. Only make, say, change; never unmakeNobody deletes from your kita over the wire. A retraction is a new statement you may honour.

And the rules that were already there do the rest: bytes don't travel (a scan or recording stays a pointer; the application serves it, with its own authorization); what to include is the application's call, so PHI stays a sender-side responsibility with a signature attached; content is data, never instructions, and now it's attributed data from a provable key.

What kapwa should take from kita's rules

kita rulewhat it fixes in kapwa
No domain nounsNo person's name in a status, no hard-coded product list. ask --to <participant>, and products are just tags. Otherwise a brokerage can't run the same binary as a practice.
Act id = hash of contentsFree, collision-proof item ids — the hash-id decision answers itself, and it's the nostr shape too.
Envelope: who · at · sourceAdd source to events (cli, hook, bridge, import). "Who is answerable" and "what produced it" are different questions; kapwa only answers the first.
Structural refusal onlyAlready ours. Keep the fold forgiving; never reject on meaning.
Measure, don't assertThe fold re-reads everything per write. Fine today; commit the script that says when it isn't.
Words from the graph's own familykita: dot, act, land, door. kapwa should pick one family and declare it, not drift between board / ledger / log.

The tempting unification, and why not

Make a kapwa log a kita log

Items become nodes, take becomes say(item, "taken-by", p), the board becomes an ask. One kernel, one format, and the bridge disappears.

  • One idea at every scale
  • The board gets kita's whole query layer
  • The kernel refuses an act that references an id it hasn't seen. With many logs arriving out of order, that is the normal case, not an error
  • kita's order is one writer's order; kapwa's is (at, writer, seq) across many
  • kita rule 7: don't build the general mechanism until the second occurrence earns it

Keep two, share the seams lean

Same envelope words, same id scheme, one pointer convention, one bridge. Each stays small enough to reimplement in an afternoon.

kernel.md already anticipates "a second machine syncing old data" (expunge leaves a scar for exactly that). When kita grows sync, revisit — kapwa's per-writer log with seq is a reasonable candidate for how.
